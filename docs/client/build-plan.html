01 Decisions taken

Four questions were settled before planning. They are recorded here because the rest of the document follows from them.

QuestionDecisionWhat it means
Build base Standalone build for Pavilion Club A new single-tenant codebase, not a tenant on the existing Turf OS platform. Turf OS is not imported, but its hard-won engineering rules are carried across deliberately — see §3.
Partner integration They call us The outside platform reads live availability from our API and pushes us confirmations. We stay the single source of truth for what is free, so overselling is structurally impossible.
Partner money Open — modelled as a receivable How the platform handles payment is not yet known. A booking they sell is recorded as confirmed but not paid to us until settled. This model is correct whichever way their payout works.

Naming conflict to resolve

The brief names Turf Town (the Indian turf-booking app); the client transcript names Townscript (an event-ticketing platform). These are different companies. The plan therefore treats the integration as a generic partner channel: any number of outside platforms plug in the same way, each with its own API key, its own colour on the calendar and its own line in the revenue report. Adding a second platform later is a database row, not a rebuild.

02 What we're building

One system, three surfaces, one shared answer to the only question that matters: is that court free at that time?

Surface one — the public site

Pavilion Club's website, rebuilt, with booking as a first-class part of it rather than a link out. A visitor lands, sees the courts, picks a date and time, pays online, and gets a confirmation. Marketing pages and the booking flow share one design and one deployment.

Surface two — the admin console

What the owner and the front desk use every day. The client described this precisely, in this order: Dashboard, Book a slot, Calendar, then reports. That order is the nav order — it reflects how the day actually runs. Detailed screen by screen in §7.

Surface three — the partner API

A documented REST API with scoped keys, so an outside platform can read availability, hold a slot, confirm it, and cancel it. Every booking that arrives this way is tagged with which partner sent it, which is what makes the month-end invoice possible.

03 The rules that hold it together

Six non-negotiables. Each one exists because breaking it produces a specific, expensive failure — usually on a Saturday evening, when the courts are full and the owner is watching. Changing one of these needs a conversation, not a commit.

  1. Availability is computed in exactly one place. One function decides what is free. The website calls it, the desk calls it, the partner API calls it. No caching in front of it. The moment there are two implementations, they will disagree, and the disagreement will be discovered by an angry customer standing on a court someone else booked.
  2. Double booking is prevented by the database, not by the code. A Postgres exclusion constraint physically rejects an overlapping booking on the same court. Application logic can have a race condition and the guarantee still holds. This is the single most important line in the schema.
  3. A booking is confirmed by the payment webhook, never by the browser. Confirming on redirect means a customer on a weak mobile network pays and loses the slot. The gateway's server-to-server webhook is the only thing that flips a booking to confirmed.
  4. Money is stored as integer paise. No floats, no decimals. ₹1,200 is 120000. Every rupee figure in the UI is formatted at the edge, never stored formatted.
  5. Prices are snapshotted onto the booking. When a booking is created, the price that applied is copied onto the row. Changing the weekend rate next month must not silently rewrite what last month's customer paid — or what a partner owes on it.
  6. A channel is data, never a branch. Website, walk-in, phone, and each partner platform are rows in a channels table. No if (source === 'townscript') anywhere in the codebase. This is what makes onboarding a second platform a five-minute job.
Where these come from

These are lifted from the Turf OS codebase in the adjacent folder, which learned several of them the hard way — including a QA pass that found bookings could be held at ₹1 because the price came from a hidden form field. We are not reusing that code, but there is no reason to relearn its lessons at Pavilion Club's expense.

05 Data model

Twelve tables. The whole system's correctness sits in about six of them.

TableHoldsNotes that matter

The line that prevents double booking

-- Required once, per database.
CREATE EXTENSION IF NOT EXISTS btree_gist;

-- Two bookings cannot overlap on the same court. Enforced by Postgres,
-- not by application code, and not by a SELECT-then-INSERT check.
ALTER TABLE bookings ADD CONSTRAINT bookings_no_overlap
  EXCLUDE USING gist (
    court_id WITH =,
    tstzrange(starts_at, ends_at, '[)') WITH &&
  ) WHERE (status IN ('held', 'confirmed'));

The '[)' bound matters: a booking ending at 19:00 and one starting at 19:00 do not overlap. The WHERE clause matters too — a cancelled booking must not keep blocking the slot.

Booking states

StatusMeaningBlocks the slot?
heldSomeone is mid-checkout. Has an expires_at, typically 10 minutes out.Yes
confirmedPaid, or accepted at the desk, or confirmed by a partner.Yes
completedThe game happened. Set by a nightly job.Past, so no
cancelledCancelled by customer, desk, partner, or hold expiry.No
no_showBooked, paid, nobody came. Kept separate from cancelled for reporting.Past, so no

06 The booking engine

How one court gets booked, and the four traps that will otherwise bite us.

From opening hours to a bookable slot

  1. Each court has an opening and closing time per weekday and a slot length (30 or 60 minutes — still to be confirmed).
  2. For a requested date, the engine generates the candidate slots for that court.
  3. It subtracts anything overlapping in bookings with status held or confirmed, and anything in blackouts.
  4. It prices each surviving slot from price_rules.
  5. It returns the list. This one function answers the website, the desk, and the partner API.

From a free slot to a confirmed booking

browse  →  HOLD (10 min, blocks the slot)  →  pay  →  webhook  →  CONFIRMED
                     │
                     └─ no payment in 10 min  →  CANCELLED (expired)

A hold is a booking row, not a separate table. That is deliberate: it is the only way one exclusion constraint can cover both holds and confirmed bookings. A separate holds table would need its own overlap check against a second table, and the two would race.

Four traps, and how each is handled

An expired hold still blocks at the database level

The constraint cannot see expires_at. So two things run: a sweeper that expires stale holds every ~30 seconds, and a retry on constraint violation 23P01 that first expires the blocking row and tries again. The retry is what makes correctness independent of the sweeper's schedule.

Overlapping bookings of different lengths can deadlock

If customers can pick consecutive slots, one person taking 19:00–21:00 while another takes 20:00–21:00 is ordinary Saturday traffic — and Postgres can deadlock (40P01) while checking the constraint. Treat a deadlock as contention: jittered exponential backoff, retry, and only report "just taken" once retries are exhausted. Without this, two people wanting overlapping hours see a 500.

Business date is not calendar date

If Pavilion Club runs past midnight, a 00:30 booking belongs to the night that is closing, not to tomorrow morning. Every report keys off business_date, computed in application code and stored on the row — Postgres will not accept AT TIME ZONE inside a generated column.

Everything is IST, stored as UTC

Timestamps stored as timestamptz, displayed in Asia/Kolkata. Never store a naive local time.

The test that proves it works

Before this ships: fire 100 simultaneous booking requests at the same slot and assert that exactly one succeeds and ninety-nine get a clean "just taken". This is an automated test that must pass on every commit, not a one-off check. If it isn't green, the product is not safe to sell.

07 Admin console, screen by screen

In the client's own order, because that order is the working day.

Dashboard Landing screen

What the owner sees on opening the laptop. Every number here needs a precise definition, because "revenue" means three different things and confusing them is how reconciliation breaks.

TileExact definition
Bookings todayConfirmed bookings whose play falls on today's business date. Not bookings created today.
Collected todaySum of payments with received_on = today. Money that actually moved, whatever day the game is.
Booked value todaySum of snapshotted slot amounts for today's play. Includes partner bookings we have not been paid for yet.
Online vs offlineOnline = website + partner platforms. Offline = walk-in + phone, entered at the desk. Split by count and by value.
Next 7 daysPer day: slots taken, slots free, percent filled. A small bar per day, clickable through to that day's grid.
Still owingConfirmed bookings for today with nothing paid. The one number the desk can act on before the customer walks off.

Book a slot The walk-in screen

Someone is standing at the counter. This screen has to be fast enough to use while talking to them.

  1. Date defaults to today. Court and time picked from a grid of what's free — the same availability function, no shortcuts.
  2. Phone number, then name. If the phone matches an existing customer, the name fills itself.
  3. Price shown from the rules, with an override that requires a reason and writes to the audit log. The price is never taken from a form field the browser sends. It is recomputed on the server.
  4. Payment mode: Cash or Card. Nothing else, per the client. Card means their own machine — we record the mode, we do not process it.
  5. One button: Payment received — block slot. Writes the booking and the payment together, in one transaction.
Learn from the neighbouring build

In Turf OS, "cash at venue" was recorded as an intention on the booking and never as a receipt, so three separate screens reported money as collected that nobody had handed over. Here, taking money writes a payments row. A booking with no payment row has not been paid, and the dashboard says so.

Calendar Month, then day

Month view. A full calendar month. Each date shows a count — "10 bookings" — and a fill indicator. Nothing more; it exists to be scanned.

Day view. Clicking a date opens that day's grid: courts across, time down. Each booked slot shows the customer, the amount, and where the booking came from — website, walk-in, phone, or the partner platform by name, in its own colour. Paid and unpaid render differently, so the desk can see at a glance who still owes. Clicking a slot opens it: reschedule, cancel with a refund quote, mark no-show.

Reports

Covered in §11. Source-wise, with the Excel export the partner invoice depends on.

Settings

  • Courts — names, opening hours per weekday, slot length, temporarily disable a court.
  • Pricing — rules by court, weekday, and time band. Weekend and evening peak rates.
  • Blackouts — block a court for maintenance or a private event.
  • Staff — add and remove users, assign roles. Removal is confirmed, and reversible.
  • Partners — add a platform, issue and revoke its API key, set its commission, see its last call.

08 Public site

Rebuilt, with booking built in rather than bolted on.

PageJob
/What Pavilion Club is, the courts, photos, location, and a booking widget above the fold showing today's next free slots.
/bookThe flow: pick date → see the grid of free slots and prices → select one or more consecutive slots → phone and name → pay.
/book/[id]Confirmation. Booking reference, court, time, amount, and what to do on arrival.

The online payment path

  1. Customer selects a slot. Server creates a hold — the slot is now blocked for 10 minutes.
  2. Server creates a Razorpay order for the recomputed price and returns it.
  3. Customer pays in the Razorpay checkout.
  4. Razorpay's webhook hits our endpoint. We verify the HMAC signature, then confirm the booking and write the payment row. This step, not the browser redirect, is what confirms the booking (R3).
  5. The browser redirect just polls the booking until it reads confirmed. If the customer's connection dies here, the booking is already safe.
  6. Webhook handling is idempotent on the gateway's event id — a duplicate delivery changes nothing.

09 Partner API

The integration the client cares most about, and the one with the most ways to go wrong.

How it authenticates

A bearer token per partner. The key is hashed at rest, bound to one channel, and carries scopes. A key can never become a user login. Rate limited to 120 requests per minute, answering 429 with a Retry-After header.

Authorization: Bearer pc_live_xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx

Endpoints

EndpointScopeDoes
GET /api/v1/availabilityavailability:readFree slots for a date, per court, with prices. The same function the desk uses.
POST /api/v1/holdsbookings:writeBlocks a slot for 10 minutes and returns a hold id. Called before they take payment.
POST /api/v1/bookingsbookings:writeConfirms a hold, with their payment reference and the customer's phone. Idempotent on their reference.
GET /api/v1/bookings/{id}bookings:readCurrent state of one booking.
POST /api/v1/bookings/{id}/cancelbookings:cancelCancels, returning the refund the policy allows.

Events we send them

Signed over timestamp.body, queued, retried with backoff to eight attempts. So their listing stays fresh when a slot is blocked at our desk:

  • slot.blocked — the desk or the website took a slot they were showing.
  • booking.cancelled — a booking they sold was cancelled on our side.
  • court.unavailable — a blackout or maintenance window was added.

The guardrails

  • Hold before payment. The partner must hold the slot before charging their customer. Without this, they will occasionally take money for a slot that went five seconds earlier. This is the single most important thing to agree with them.
  • No cached inventory. If they mirror our availability into their own database and sell from the mirror, R1 is broken and double bookings become inevitable. If they insist on caching, the fallback is to allocate them a fixed block of slots they own exclusively — less revenue, but safe.
  • Every response carries a machine-readable code. They match on code, never on the message text. slot_taken, missing_scope, rate_limited, and so on.
  • Sandbox keys. pc_test_… behaves identically and marks its bookings as test data, so they can integrate without polluting the revenue report.
Questions to put to the partner platform
  1. At checkout, do you read our availability live, or from a cache you refresh periodically?
  2. Can you call a hold endpoint before taking payment, and confirm after? (This is the safe pattern and we need a yes.)
  3. Who holds the customer's money, and what is the payout cycle to Pavilion Club?
  4. What commission do you take, and is it deducted before or after payout?
  5. Do you pass us the customer's name and phone number? The desk needs it at the gate.
  6. When a customer cancels on your side, do you call our cancel endpoint?
  7. Do you want webhooks from us when a slot is blocked at our desk?
  8. Is there a sandbox we can test against before go-live?

10 Money and settlement

Four ways money reaches Pavilion Club, and only three of them arrive on the day.

MethodArrivesRecorded as
Online (Razorpay)Settled to the bank in T+2Payment row on webhook confirmation. Counts as collected on the day the customer paid.
Cash at the deskImmediately, into the tillPayment row written by whoever took it, on the business date they took it. Feeds the cash handover.
Card at the deskThrough their own machinePayment row, method card. We record the mode; we do not process the card.
Partner platformLater, on invoiceBooking confirmed, no payment row. A receivable against that partner until settled.

The settlement cycle

partner books  →  PENDING   (confirmed, unpaid to us)
month ends     →  INVOICED  (export the sheet, send it to them)
they pay       →  SETTLED   (marked in Settings → Partners, with the amount and date)

This model is correct whichever way the partner's payouts turn out to work. If they remit instantly, a booking simply moves to settled the same day. If they remit monthly on invoice — what the client described — the outstanding balance sits visibly on the dashboard until it clears.

Why this matters more than it sounds

If partner bookings were counted as "collected", the daily cash figure would never match the bank, and nobody would trust any number in the system. Keeping booked value and collected as separate figures everywhere is what keeps the reports honest.

11 Reports and export

The client's stated purpose: "source-wise they select and export, there should be an Excel sheet which they can download and go and ask them, this is the booking happened through you, give me the money."

Source-wise booking report

Pick a date range, defaulting to the current calendar month. One row per channel, showing bookings, hours sold, booked value, collected, commission, and outstanding. Website, walk-in, phone and each partner all appear as their own line. A total row at the bottom that reconciles.

The Excel export

The same range, exported as .xlsx, with two sheets:

  • Summary — the channel totals above, ready to attach to an email.
  • Bookings — one row per booking: reference, date, time, court, customer name and phone, amount, commission, net owed, their payment reference, and settlement status. This is the sheet that goes to the partner as evidence.

Other reports

  • Occupancy — percent filled by day of week and hour, over a chosen period. The report that tells the owner Wednesday afternoons are dead and should be discounted.
  • Daily close — the 11:30pm screen. Bookings, collected by method, expected cash in the till, and what is still owed.
  • Cash handover — what the system expects from a shift versus what the staff member declares. The declared figure starts empty; pre-filling it with the expected amount makes every reconciliation come out clean and worthless.

12 Roles, alerts, and running it

Who can do what

RoleCanCannot
OwnerEverything, including pricing, staff, partners, and revenue.—
ManagerAll bookings, daily close, reports, blackouts.Change pricing, issue API keys, remove staff.
Desk staffCreate and cancel bookings, take payment, see today.See revenue reports, override prices without a reason, export data.

Every cancellation, price override and refund writes an audit row naming the user. This is not bureaucracy — it is what settles the argument when a slot goes missing on a busy evening.

Notifications

  • Customer — WhatsApp confirmation on booking, with SMS fallback if the WhatsApp send fails. A reminder the morning of the game. Cancellation confirmation with the refund amount.
  • Venue — a daily summary to the owner. Optionally an alert when a partner booking arrives.
  • Messages go through an outbox with retry and backoff, never sent inline during a request. A slow messaging provider must never slow down a booking.

Before it holds real money

  • Nightly database backup plus continuous WAL archiving to off-box storage.
  • A restore rehearsal onto a clean machine. An untested backup is not a backup, and Saturday 8pm is both the peak and the worst possible time to discover that.
  • Error tracking (Sentry) and uptime monitoring with an alert to a phone.
  • Secrets held outside the repository. HTTPS everywhere, HSTS on.
  • Rate limiting on OTP endpoints — the obvious abuse target.
